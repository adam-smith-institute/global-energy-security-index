Methodology

The index scores every country on four components of energy security: dispatchability, diversity, import security and ability to pay. Each is scored from 0 to 100, and the overall index is their average. The 2026 index uses 2024 data and ranks the 187 countries and territories with a score on all four. Each step below sets out the formula, then works it through for the UK.

  1. 1Dispatchability
  2. 2Diversity
  3. 3Import security
  4. 4Ability to pay
  5. 5Overall index

Component 1: Dispatchability

The share of a country’s electricity that can be generated on demand, whatever the weather. Hydro, nuclear, coal, gas, oil, geothermal, biomass and waste count in full; wind, solar, and tide and wave do not.

C1=100×G−Gw−Gs−GtG
(1)

where G is total electricity generation and Gw, Gs and Gt are generation from wind, solar, and tide and wave, all in billion kWh.

United Kingdom, 2024

The UK generated G=267.980 billion kWh, of which Gw=82.855 came from wind, Gs=14.796 from solar and Gt=0.010 from tide and wave, so

C1=100×267.980−82.855−14.796−0.010267.980=100×170.319267.980=63.56

Just under two-thirds of UK electricity was dispatchable, which ranks the UK on this component.

Data: EIA international data, electricity net generation, 2024. EIA figures are used unmodified, except Lesotho’s tide and wave generation, corrected to zero.

Component 2: Diversity

How evenly a country’s primary-energy consumption is spread across five categories: petroleum and other liquids, natural gas, coal, nuclear, and renewables and other. Concentration is measured with a Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI), then rescaled so that an even mix scores 100 and reliance on a single source scores 0.

Component 3: Import security

How exposed a country is to imported energy. Net import dependence is weighted by a risk factor that rises when a country relies on few suppliers, when it is politically unstable itself, and when its suppliers are. A net exporter scores 100: it meets its own needs in aggregate, can re-route supply at home in a crisis, and can use its exports to help allies.

where Ec and Ep are primary-energy consumption and production; sj is supplier j’s share, by value, of the country’s imports across 31 energy categories; and D is the country’s 2024 World Bank political stability score (WGI) divided by 100, with Dj the same for supplier j. So I is net import dependence, H the concentration of suppliers, S their import-weighted stability and R the import-risk factor. A net exporter has I=0.

United Kingdom, 2024

The UK consumed Ec=6.4106 and produced Ep=3.5729 quadrillion Btu (6.76 and 3.77 EJ), so

I=6.4106−3.57296.4106=0.4427

Its identified energy imports, worth $84.4bn, came from 115 suppliers. Norway alone supplied $26.3bn, a share of 0.3117, and the squared shares of all 115 sum to H=0.1674. The UK’s own stability score gives D=0.7030, and its suppliers’ import-weighted score gives S=0.6937. Then

R=0.40+0.20(0.1674)+0.20(1−0.7030)+0.20(1−0.6937)=0.5541C3=100×(1−0.4427×0.5541)=75.47

which ranks the UK on this component.

Import shares exclude trade recorded as ‘Other Asia, nes’, and S counts only suppliers with a WGI score. EIA puts Guyana’s energy production at almost nothing, despite its oil exports; it is treated as a net exporter.

Component 4: Ability to pay

GDP per capita, a proxy for a country’s ability to pay a premium for energy in a global shortage. Net exporters are scored too: if their own production stopped, they would have to buy on the world market. Taking logarithms damps the effect of a few very rich countries, and the result is scaled between the poorest country in the dataset (0) and the richest (100).

C4=100×lny−lnyminlnymax−lnymin
(9)

where y is GDP per capita in constant 2015 US dollars. The lowest value in the dataset, ymin, is Burundi’s $268.70; the highest, ymax, is Bermuda’s $116,513.54.

United Kingdom, 2024

UK GDP per capita was $47,900.97, so

C4=100×ln47,900.97−ln268.70ln116,513.54−ln268.70=100×10.7769−5.593611.6658−5.5936=100×5.18336.0722=85.36

which ranks the UK on this component.

Data: World Bank GDP per capita (constant 2015 US$), 2024.

On the heat map, scores fall into fixed ten-point bands applied to every country: Most secure 90–100, Moderately secure 80–89.9, Borderline 70–79.9, Moderately insecure 60–69.9 and Most insecure below 60.

Units

Index inputs follow EIA units. The Energy Institute equivalents above use the conversion factors in the Statistical Review of World Energy 2026:

  • 1 exajoule (EJ) = 948 trillion Btu, so 1 quadrillion Btu ≈ 1.055 EJ
  • 1 billion kWh = 1 terawatt-hour (TWh); 1 EJ ≈ 278 TWh
  • 1 kWh = 3,412 Btu = 3,600 kJ
  • 1 billion m³ natural gas = 36 PJ = 0.86 million toe
